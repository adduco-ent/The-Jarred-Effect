Discipline

6 steps

One tiny step

10 min

Do the smallest daily step toward your Ask, whenever today allows.

Why it helps

Small steps you actually take teach your gut that this is real. A big plan you skip teaches it the opposite.

Walk me through it

  1. Pick the smallest action that still moves your Ask forward.
  2. Do it whenever today allows, wherever it fits.
  3. Stop when the time’s up, even if you want to keep going. Leave yourself wanting more.

If you get stuck

If it feels too big to start, cut it in half, then in half again. Two minutes is still a step.

In real life made-up example

Maya wanted to write a book and had been “about to start” for two years. Her tiny step: one page before she opened her phone in the morning. Some pages were bad. A few were good. After three weeks she had twenty-one pages, and for the first time the book felt like something she was doing instead of something she was waiting on.

Find today’s window

3 min

Look at today and pick the moment most likely to work for your step. Tomorrow it can be a different one.

Why it helps

A fixed time breaks the first day your schedule changes. Picking today’s best window each morning keeps the step alive on real days, not ideal ones.

Walk me through it

  1. Look at today as it actually is: meetings, kids, energy.
  2. Find the one moment most likely to have ten quiet minutes.
  3. Put it somewhere you’ll see it: a calendar block, a sticky note, a phone reminder.

If you get stuck

If no window looks free, pick the least bad one and do the small version there.

In real life made-up example

Marcus kept setting 6 a.m. workouts and missing them after late nights with the baby. He switched to choosing each morning. Monday it was lunch. Tuesday it was after bath time. Wednesday it was ten minutes in the parking lot before work. Different every day, but it happened most days, which 6 a.m. never managed.

Your bad-day backup

5 min

Write the version of your step you’ll do when the day goes sideways.

Why it helps

Most streaks don’t end on a normal day. They end on the bad one. Deciding your backup ahead of time means the bad day still has a step that fits.

Walk me through it

  1. Think of what a sideways day looks like for you: no sleep, sick kid, long shift.
  2. Shrink your step until it would still be doable on that day.
  3. Write it in one line: “If the day goes sideways, I’ll ___.”

If you get stuck

If the backup still feels like too much, cut it again. The backup should feel almost too easy.

In real life made-up example

Priya’s step was a 20-minute run. Her backup: put on her running shoes and walk to the corner and back. The first time she needed it was a Thursday with a migraine coming on. Five minutes, slow, done. She noticed it kept the week from collapsing, because Friday didn’t feel like starting over.

Keep one promise

10 min

Make one small promise to yourself this morning, and keep it by tonight.

Why it helps

Trust in yourself is built the same way as trust in anyone else: small promises, kept. One kept promise a day adds up fast.

Walk me through it

  1. This morning, pick one small thing you can finish today.
  2. Say it to yourself as a promise: “By tonight, I will ___.”
  3. Keep it. Before bed, notice that you did.

If you get stuck

If you keep breaking it, the promise is too big. Make it smaller until keeping it is likely.

In real life made-up example

Theo was known at work for following through and at home for “I’ll get to it.” He started making one morning promise to himself: book the dentist, call his mom back, fix the cabinet door. Small stuff. By Friday he’d kept five in a row, and he caught himself saying “I’ll do it today” and meaning it.

One line tonight

3 min

Tonight, write one line about what you actually did toward your Ask today.

Why it helps

Your mind remembers what you didn’t do. A written line about what you did shows the truth, and over time it becomes proof you can lean on.

Walk me through it

  1. Keep one note or notebook just for these lines.
  2. Each night, write one honest line about what you did toward your Ask.
  3. Include how it felt if you want, but keep it to one line.

If you get stuck

If you did nothing today, write “not yet.” That’s still a line, and tomorrow is a new one.

In real life made-up example

Elena felt like she was getting nowhere with her Spanish. She started a one-line note: “10 min app.” “Ordered in Spanish, panicked, did it anyway.” “Not yet.” A month later she scrolled back and counted 24 days with something on them. The feeling of getting nowhere didn’t match the lines, so she started trusting the lines.

Start before you feel like it

5 min

Start today’s step before you feel ready. Give it five minutes, then decide whether to keep going.

Why it helps

Waiting to feel ready keeps you waiting. Starting usually brings the motivation, not the other way around. Five minutes is enough to find out.

Walk me through it

  1. Pick the first physical action of your step: open the laptop, put on the shoes.
  2. Set a five-minute timer and start, ready or not.
  3. When it rings, decide honestly: keep going, or stop here. Both count.

If you get stuck

If even five minutes feels impossible, make it two, and make the first action tiny.

In real life made-up example

Jordan dreaded starting his business plan. He set a five-minute timer and opened the doc, telling himself he could stop when it rang. He wrote three messy sentences. When the timer went off he was mid-thought, so he kept going for twenty minutes. Not every day went that way, but every day started.

Desire

6 steps

Why, three times

5 min

Write why you want your Ask. Then ask “why?” about your answer, twice more.

Why it helps

Wants that sit on the surface fade fast. The deeper why is what pulls you through the low days, because it’s about how you want to feel, not only what you want to get.

Walk me through it

  1. Write your Ask at the top of a page or a note.
  2. Under it, write why you want it, in one honest line.
  3. Ask “why does that matter?” about that line, and write the answer. Do it once more.

If you get stuck

If the answers sound like what you’re supposed to say, ask: what would I want here if no one would ever know?

In real life made-up example

Sam wanted to run a 5K. First why: “to get in shape.” Second: “because I’m out of breath chasing my kids.” Third: “because I want to be the dad who’s in the game, not on the bench.” That third one went on a sticky note on his running shoes. On days he didn’t feel like it, he didn’t read “get in shape.” He read the bench line, and he went.

The want list

5 min

Write five things you want that have nothing to do with work.

Why it helps

When work takes everything, wanting starts to feel selfish, and then it goes quiet. Naming what you want outside of work brings your desire back to life.

Walk me through it

  1. Set a timer for five minutes.
  2. Write five things you want that have nothing to do with work, money or other people’s needs.
  3. Circle the one that makes you feel something.

If you get stuck

If nothing comes, finish this line five times: “It would be nice to…”

In real life made-up example

Aisha couldn’t think of a single thing at first. Then: “a slow Sunday,” “a pottery class,” “to see the ocean in winter,” “to read a whole book,” “a garden.” She circled pottery. It surprised her how much she wanted it. She looked up a class that night just to see, and saved it.

Picture a normal day after

3 min

Close your eyes and picture an ordinary day once your Ask is real. Notice where you are and how your body feels.

Why it helps

Big goals feel far away when you only picture the finish line. Picturing an ordinary day makes it feel real, and it tells you what you’re actually after.

Walk me through it

  1. Close your eyes somewhere quiet.
  2. Picture a normal weekday once your Ask is real: waking up, the morning, your body.
  3. Notice one detail that stands out, and write it down.

If you get stuck

If the picture won’t come, start with your coffee. Where are you drinking it, and how do you feel?

In real life made-up example

Luis pictured life after paying off his debt. Not a party, just a Tuesday. What stood out was the morning: opening his banking app without his stomach dropping. That small detail became his why. He wrote “no stomach drop” at the top of his budget sheet.

One thing just because

15 min

Do one thing today only because you enjoy it.

Why it helps

If everything you do has to earn its place, you lose touch with what you enjoy, and then with what you want. Doing one thing just because it’s good reconnects you.

Walk me through it

  1. Pick one thing you enjoy that has no goal: music, a walk, cooking, a show.
  2. Give it fifteen minutes today.
  3. Don’t multitask it. Let it just be good.

If you get stuck

If it feels like a waste of time, that’s the feeling this step is working on. Do it anyway, and notice how you feel after.

In real life made-up example

Grace couldn’t remember the last thing she did just because. She played an old album loud while she made dinner, the one from college. Fifteen minutes, singing badly. Her daughter joined in. Nothing productive happened, and she felt more like herself than she had in weeks.

Drop one should

5 min

Cross off one task that’s a “should”, not a “want”, and don’t replace it.

Why it helps

A day full of shoulds leaves no room for wants. Crossing off one should, on purpose, makes space and shows you that most of them were optional.

Walk me through it

  1. Look at your list for today or this week.
  2. Find one task that’s there because you “should”, not because it matters to you.
  3. Cross it off and leave the space empty.

If you get stuck

If everything feels mandatory, ask: what happens if this waits a week? Usually, not much.

In real life made-up example

Dev had “reorganize the garage” on his list for six weekends straight. Nobody had asked him to. He crossed it off. That Saturday, the hour he’d have spent feeling guilty about the garage, he spent teaching his son to ride a bike. The garage was fine.

Finish the sentence

2 min

Finish this sentence in writing: “When this is real, I’ll finally feel ___.”

Why it helps

Your Ask is really about a feeling. Naming the feeling tells you what you’re chasing, and sometimes it shows you a shorter path to it.

Walk me through it

  1. Write the sentence: “When this is real, I’ll finally feel ___.”
  2. Fill in the blank with the first honest word.
  3. Ask: is there one small way to feel a bit of that this week?

If you get stuck

If the word that comes is about other people, like “they’ll respect me,” ask how you’d feel then, and write that.

In real life made-up example

Nina filled it in: “When I get the promotion, I’ll finally feel like I’m not behind.” Behind who? She realized she’d been comparing herself to her old roommate for ten years. She still wanted the promotion, but she stopped checking her roommate’s LinkedIn, and some of that behind feeling eased right away.

Faith

6 steps

One proof it’s possible

5 min

Find one person who already did something like your Ask, and note how they started.

Why it helps

Doubt says “people like me don’t do this.” One real example of someone who did is hard evidence against it.

Walk me through it

  1. Think of what your Ask looks like when someone else has done it.
  2. Find one person, known or not, who did something similar.
  3. Write down how they started, not how they finished.

If you get stuck

If you can’t find anyone, look for someone who did something just as hard in a different area. The starting part is usually the same.

In real life made-up example

Omar wanted to open a food truck at 45 and kept thinking he was too late. He found an interview with a woman who started hers at 52 with one menu item and a borrowed trailer. He wrote down “one menu item.” That night he started testing his one best dish on friends.

Act with the doubt still there

10 min

Take one step today while one doubt is still open.

Why it helps

Waiting for the doubt to leave keeps you stuck, because it usually leaves after you act. Moving with the doubt still there is how it starts to shrink.

Walk me through it

  1. Name the doubt in one line.
  2. Pick one step you can take today anyway.
  3. Take it, and let the doubt come along for the ride.

If you get stuck

If the doubt is loud, make the step smaller. You’re practicing moving with it, not beating it.

In real life made-up example

Hannah doubted her photos were good enough to share. She posted one anyway, doubt and all, with a short caption. Eleven people liked it, one asked about prints. The doubt didn’t vanish, but it got a little quieter, and she posted another the next week.

The evidence list

5 min

Write three times something worked out that you once doubted.

Why it helps

Your mind keeps a long list of what went wrong and a short one of what went right. Writing down what worked out evens the record.

Walk me through it

  1. Think back over the last five or ten years.
  2. Write three things that worked out even though you doubted them at the time.
  3. Next to each, write what you did that helped.

If you get stuck

If nothing feels big enough, start small: a test you passed, a hard talk that went fine, a move you survived.

In real life made-up example

Kenji felt sure his new job would go badly. He wrote his evidence list: “Moved across the country alone. Passed the certification on the second try. Fixed things with my brother.” Next to each he wrote what he did. Reading it back, he saw the pattern: he’s someone who figures things out. He kept the list in his wallet.

Ask for a yes

10 min

Ask someone for something you want, and let them answer.

Why it helps

Many people never ask, so the answer is always no. Asking, and letting the other person decide, gives life the chance to say yes.

Walk me through it

  1. Pick one thing you want that someone else can say yes to.
  2. Write the ask in one or two clear sentences.
  3. Send it or say it, then let them answer. Don’t answer for them.

If you get stuck

If you’re afraid of the no, remember it leaves you where you are now. Nothing is lost by asking.

In real life made-up example

Rosa had wanted to work from home on Fridays for a year and never asked. She wrote two sentences to her manager: the ask, and how she’d stay reachable. The answer was “let’s try it for a month.” She’d spent a year assuming no.

A window for worry

10 min

Give your doubts ten minutes on paper. Then close the notebook and do your next thing.

Why it helps

Worry leaks into the whole day when it has no place to go. Giving it a set window lets you hear it out, then get back to living.

Walk me through it

  1. Set a ten-minute timer.
  2. Write every “what if” in your head, without arguing with them.
  3. When the timer ends, close the notebook and do your next thing.

If you get stuck

If a worry comes back later, tell it: “You have a spot tomorrow.” Write it down for the next window.

In real life made-up example

Ben worried about money all day, in meetings, in the car, at bedtime. He gave it ten minutes at 7 p.m. with a notebook. He wrote fourteen what ifs the first night. By the fourth night it was six, and two of them had turned into to-dos he could actually do something about.

What if it works?

3 min

Write what happens if your Ask goes right.

Why it helps

Your mind spends a lot of time on what could go wrong. Spending a few minutes on what happens if it goes right gives your hope something to hold.

Walk me through it

  1. Write your Ask at the top.
  2. Write what happens if it goes right: a month after, a year after.
  3. Notice one part that makes you feel lighter.

If you get stuck

If “what if it works” feels silly, write it like you’re telling a friend’s story, not your own.

In real life made-up example

Leah had only ever pictured her bakery going under. She wrote what happens if it works: regulars by name, her mom helping on Saturdays, paying off the oven. Reading it back, her chest unclenched. She pinned it above the counter where she does the books.

Friction

6 steps

The 2:47 check

1 min

Set an alarm for mid-afternoon. When it goes off, ask: What am I feeling in my gut right now? Is it what I’d tell someone? Can I feel it and still do my next task?

Why it helps

Mid-afternoon is when the day wears thin and feelings get covered up. A short check at that moment shows you what you’re carrying and whether you can carry it and still keep going.

Walk me through it

  1. Set a daily alarm for mid-afternoon. Label it “2:47”.
  2. When it goes off, ask: what am I feeling in my gut right now?
  3. Ask: is it what I’d tell someone if they asked?
  4. Ask: can I feel this and still do my next task?

If you get stuck

If you can’t name the feeling, pick the closest word: tired, tense, flat, okay. Close enough counts.

In real life made-up example

Tariq’s 2:47 alarm caught him mid-spreadsheet. Gut: anxious about the client call. What he’d tell someone: “fine.” Could he feel it and still make the call? Yes. He made the call with his stomach still a bit tight. He noticed that he’d been treating “anxious” like a stop sign, when it was really just weather.

Name the hard part

5 min

Write the one part of your Ask you’ve been avoiding.

Why it helps

The part you avoid quietly runs the whole Ask. Naming it takes away some of its power and shows you what you actually need to work on.

Walk me through it

  1. Write your Ask.
  2. Ask: what part of this do I keep stepping around?
  3. Write it in plain words, even if it feels uncomfortable.

If you get stuck

If you’re not sure, look at what you keep putting off. The avoided part usually hides behind “later.”

In real life made-up example

Chloe wanted to grow her design business. The part she kept avoiding: talking about price. She’d send portfolios, chat, then go quiet when money came up. Naming it was humbling. It was also the first time she could see what was actually holding her back, and she started practicing her rate out loud in the car.

Two minutes with it

2 min

When fear shows up today, don’t fix it. Feel it for two minutes, then do the next thing.

Why it helps

Fear usually passes faster when you feel it instead of fighting it. Two minutes with it, then moving, teaches you that fear doesn’t have to decide.

Walk me through it

  1. When fear shows up, stop what you’re doing.
  2. Notice where you feel it in your body. Breathe slowly for two minutes.
  3. Then do the next thing, with the fear still there if it is.

If you get stuck

If two minutes feels too long, take three slow breaths or a quick five-minute walk, then move.

In real life made-up example

Andre felt sick before every sales call. He started sitting with it: hand on his chest, breathing, two minutes. He didn’t try to talk himself out of it. Then he dialed. The fear was still there sometimes, but it stopped running the show. He made more calls that week than the month before.

The price check

5 min

Answer in writing: What will this really cost me, and will I still pay it on a bad day?

Why it helps

Every Ask costs something. Naming the cost ahead of time, and checking whether you’ll pay it on a bad day, keeps you from quitting when the price shows up.

Walk me through it

  1. Write your Ask.
  2. Answer: what will this really cost me? Time, comfort, money, sleep, something else.
  3. Answer: will I still pay it on a bad day? If not, how could the step get smaller?

If you get stuck

If the cost feels too high, it doesn’t mean the Ask is wrong. It may mean the step is too big.

In real life made-up example

Mei wanted to learn to code after work. The price: evenings and some TV. Would she pay it on a bad day? Honestly, not an hour. So she set her step at twenty minutes, three nights a week. That was a price she’d pay even tired, and she kept paying it.

Five more minutes

5 min

When you want to quit today’s step, stay five more minutes.

Why it helps

The moment you want to quit is often right before something gets easier. Staying a few minutes past it builds the muscle of holding steady.

Walk me through it

  1. Notice the moment you want to stop today’s step.
  2. Set a timer for five more minutes.
  3. Keep going until it rings, then stop with no guilt.

If you get stuck

If five minutes feels impossible, try one more minute. It’s about staying a little past the urge.

In real life made-up example

Isaac always stopped his guitar practice at the hard chord change. He started adding five minutes right when he wanted to quit. The chord was still clumsy. But he noticed his urge to stop came earlier on tired days, and staying past it taught him the urge wasn’t a real limit.

What did I miss?

5 min

Pick one thing that didn’t go your way this week and write what it showed you.

Why it helps

A setback hurts. Looking at it with curiosity instead of blame turns it into information you can use next time.

Walk me through it

  1. Pick one thing that didn’t go your way this week.
  2. Write what happened, in one line, without judging it.
  3. Ask: what did this show me? Write one thing.

If you get stuck

If it still stings too much to look at, write how it felt first. Then come back to what it showed you tomorrow.

In real life made-up example

Fatima’s pitch to a big client fell flat. Her first thought was “I’m not good at this.” Instead she wrote: “Pitched for 20 minutes. Never asked what they actually needed.” That was the lesson. Her next pitch started with three questions, and it went very differently.

Ambition

6 steps

The next level

5 min

Write what the next level of your Ask looks like, one step past where you are now.

Why it helps

The far goal can feel unreachable. The next level, one step past where you are, is something you can see and start on today.

Walk me through it

  1. Write where you are with your Ask right now, honestly.
  2. Write what one level up looks like: not the dream, just the next step.
  3. Pick one thing that moves you toward that level this week.

If you get stuck

If you can’t see the next level, ask someone a step ahead of you what came right after where you are.

In real life made-up example

Cole could run two miles and dreamed of a half marathon. The next level wasn’t 13 miles. It was three miles without stopping. He wrote that down, and added half a mile every week. It felt doable in a way the half marathon never had.

One stretch

15 min

Do one thing a little bigger than comfortable.

Why it helps

Growth lives a little past comfortable. One stretch shows you that your limits are further out than you thought.

Walk me through it

  1. Pick one thing that feels a size too big for you.
  2. Do the smallest real version of it today.
  3. Notice how it feels afterward, whatever the outcome.

If you get stuck

If it feels too big to do today, draft it today and do it tomorrow. Drafting is part of the stretch.

In real life made-up example

Jasmine wanted to work with a well-known brand and assumed they were out of reach. She wrote the pitch, read it three times and sent it. They didn’t reply. She was surprised by how little that hurt, and the next “out of reach” email felt easier to send.

Resting or settling?

3 min

Ask yourself: am I resting, or am I settling? Comfort is a place to rest, not a place to live.

Why it helps

Rest and settling can look the same from the outside. Telling them apart helps you rest without guilt, and notice when comfort has become a hiding place.

Walk me through it

  1. When you’re taking it easy, pause and ask: am I resting, or settling?
  2. Resting: I chose it, it’s short, I feel better after.
  3. Settling: I’m drifting, avoiding, and I don’t feel better.

If you get stuck

If you can’t tell, ask how you feel after. Rest refills you. Settling leaves you flat.

In real life made-up example

Raj spent most evenings on the couch and couldn’t tell if he was recharging or hiding. He asked the question for a week. Monday and Wednesday: resting, he felt better after. The other nights: settling, he was avoiding his side project. He kept two couch nights and gave the rest twenty minutes on the project.

Raise one standard

10 min

Pick one small thing and do it a notch better than usual.

Why it helps

You don’t need a giant leap to grow. Raising one small standard, a notch at a time, changes how you see yourself.

Walk me through it

  1. Pick one thing you do every day or every week.
  2. Decide what a notch better looks like, specifically.
  3. Do it that way today.

If you get stuck

If it feels like nitpicking, pick something you care about. The standard should feel like respect, not pressure.

In real life made-up example

Bri sent client emails fast and fixed typos after. She raised one standard: read each email once before sending. It took an extra minute. Clients started replying “thanks for the clear update.” It was small, but it changed how she thought of her own work.

Say it out loud

5 min

Tell one person what you want next.

Why it helps

A want you keep to yourself is easy to drop. Saying it out loud makes it real, and sometimes the person you tell can help.

Walk me through it

  1. Pick one person you trust.
  2. Tell them in one sentence what you want next.
  3. You don’t need a plan yet. Just say it.

If you get stuck

If saying it feels too exposed, write it somewhere you’ll see every day first. Say it out loud next week.

In real life made-up example

Noah told his sister over dinner: “I want to launch my podcast by spring.” She didn’t laugh. She asked what it was about, then said, “My coworker does audio editing.” He had kept that want to himself for a year, and within a week it had a name, a date and a possible helper.

Try one new thing

15 min

Try something you’ve never done that connects to your Ask. Every try teaches you something, however it goes.

Why it helps

Trying new things is how you find out what you like and what you’re good at. Every try teaches you something, however it goes.

Walk me through it

  1. Pick one new thing connected to your Ask: a class, an email, a tool, a route.
  2. Do the beginner version.
  3. Afterward, write one thing you learned about yourself.

If you get stuck

If nothing feels right, pick the one that makes you a little curious. Curiosity is enough.

In real life made-up example

Imani wanted to change careers but didn’t know to what. She tried a free beginner UX workshop. She didn’t love the software, but she loved interviewing users. That told her more than months of reading job posts. Her next try was a research course.

Acceptance

6 steps

Count what’s done

5 min

Write five things you’ve already built or finished.

Why it helps

When you only look at what’s left to do, you forget how far you’ve come. Counting what’s done gives you solid ground to stand on.

Walk me through it

  1. Set a five-minute timer.
  2. Write five things you’ve already built, finished or handled.
  3. Read them back slowly.

If you get stuck

If five feels like a lot, go small: a hard week you got through counts.

In real life made-up example

Victor felt like he’d done nothing with his thirties. He wrote: paid off the car, kept the same job through layoffs, learned to cook, stayed close with his dad, quit smoking. Reading it back, he laughed. It was a lot. He kept the list on his phone for the low days.

Decide what’s enough

2 min

Before you start today, decide what “enough” looks like. Stop there.

Why it helps

Without a finish line, the day never feels done, and you never feel like you did enough. Deciding what “enough” is before you start gives you permission to stop.

Walk me through it

  1. Before you start today, look at what you want to get done.
  2. Decide what counts as enough for today.
  3. When you reach it, stop. Notice that it’s done.

If you get stuck

If you keep moving the line, write it down before you start. Written lines are harder to move.

In real life made-up example

Sofia ended every workday feeling behind, even on good days. She started writing “Enough today = ___” on a sticky note each morning. Thursday it said: “three emails, one proposal draft.” She got there by 4 p.m., closed the laptop and went for a walk. That felt strange at first, then good.

Mark a win

5 min

Mark one win from this week on purpose. Tell someone, or treat yourself.

Why it helps

Wins you don’t notice don’t count in your head. Marking one on purpose teaches your mind that progress is happening.

Walk me through it

  1. Look back at this week.
  2. Pick one win, any size.
  3. Mark it: tell someone, write it down, or treat yourself.

If you get stuck

If nothing feels like a win, pick something you did even though you didn’t want to. That’s a win.

In real life made-up example

Eli finished the first draft of his grant proposal and almost moved straight to the next task. Instead he texted a friend: “Draft done.” She replied with three exclamation points and a cake emoji. It took ten seconds and he smiled for an hour.

One slow thing

15 min

Do one thing slowly today, with no goal attached.

Why it helps

If you truly can’t move without them, name the key you want back and ask for it clearly.

In real life made-up example

Eric had been waiting three months for his business partner to start the website. He bought the domain himself that afternoon and set up a simple page. His partner saw it and said, “Oh good, let’s go.” The wait had been the only thing in the way.

Making room

7 steps

What am I still carrying?

10 min

List what you’re still holding on to: a role, a label, a story, a hurt, a hope, a thing you keep paying for.

Why it helps

You can’t let go of what you haven’t named. Writing it down turns a vague heaviness into a short list you can actually work with.

Walk me through it

  1. Set a ten-minute timer.
  2. List what you’re still holding on to: roles, labels, stories, hurts, hopes, things you keep paying for.
  3. Circle the one that feels heaviest. You don’t have to do anything with it yet.

If you get stuck

If nothing comes, ask: what do I keep bringing up, or what do I avoid thinking about? Both point to something you’re carrying.

In real life made-up example

Kofi thought he’d moved on from his old job. Then he wrote his list: the title he still used in his bio, a grudge against his old manager, a business card stack in his drawer. Seeing it on paper, he realized he’d been living half in his old life. He circled the grudge.

Thank it, then let it go

10 min

Pick one thing from your list. Write what it gave you, thank it, and write one line of goodbye.

Why it helps

Letting go feels like loss when you skip the thank you. Honoring what something gave you lets you close it with respect instead of regret.

Walk me through it

  1. Pick one thing from your list.
  2. Write what it gave you: a skill, a lesson, a season of your life.
  3. Write “thank you”, then one line of goodbye.

If you get stuck

If it only ever hurt you, write what it taught you instead. That counts as what it gave you.

In real life made-up example

Sadie had kept her old dance shoes for six years without dancing. She wrote: “You gave me my twenties and my best friends. Thank you. I’m not that dancer anymore, and that’s okay.” She gave the shoes to her niece. It felt like closing a good book instead of losing something.

Retire an old label

5 min

Write one label you’ve outgrown. Then write who you’re becoming instead, in words you can believe.

Why it helps

Old labels quietly decide what you’ll even try. Retiring one, on purpose, gives the newer version of you room to act.

Walk me through it

  1. Write one label you’ve outgrown, exactly as you say it.
  2. Ask: is this still true, or just familiar?
  3. Write who you’re becoming instead, in words you can believe today.

If you get stuck

If the new version feels fake, make it smaller: “I’m learning to…” is always true.

In real life made-up example

Arjun had called himself “not a people person” since school. He wrote it down and noticed he’d led three projects this year and liked it. He rewrote it: “I’m someone who’s good with people one on one.” He signed up to mentor a new hire.

Stop paying for the past

5 min

For one thing you keep because of what you’ve put in, ask: would I start this today? If not, what is keeping it costing me now?

Why it helps

Time and money already spent are gone either way. Asking “would I start this today?” shows you what keeping it is really costing you now.

Walk me through it

  1. Pick one thing you keep mostly because of what you’ve put in.
  2. Ask: if I were starting fresh today, would I choose this?
  3. Write what keeping it costs you each week: time, money, energy.

If you get stuck

If you can’t decide, you don’t have to. Write the cost down, and look again in a week.

In real life made-up example

Beth had spent two years and most of her savings on an online store that barely sold. Would she start it today? No. It cost her every Sunday and a knot in her stomach. She didn’t shut it down that day. She set a date, and her Sundays started to feel like hers again.

Forgive to free yourself

10 min

Write a letter you’ll never send to someone you’re still carrying. Say what happened, how it felt, and what you’re putting down.

Why it helps

Holding a grudge keeps you tied to the person who hurt you. Forgiving, for your own sake, puts the weight down without saying what happened was okay.

Walk me through it

  1. Pick one person you’re still carrying.
  2. Write them a letter you will never send: what happened, how it felt, what it cost you.
  3. End with what you’re putting down, then close the page.

If you get stuck

If you’re not ready to forgive, don’t force it. Write the letter anyway. Saying it fully is often the first step. If it involves abuse or trauma, talk to someone you trust or a professional.

In real life made-up example

Malik was still angry at a friend who’d bailed on a business they’d started together. He wrote the letter, all of it, then the last line: “I’m done carrying this around for you.” He didn’t send it or call him. He just noticed, a few days later, that he’d stopped rehearsing the argument in the shower.

Let it hurt a little

5 min

When grief or missing shows up for something you let go, give it five minutes. Feel it, then go back to your day.

Why it helps

Even the right goodbye can hurt. Letting grief move through in small doses keeps it from building up or pulling you back to what you left.

Walk me through it

  1. When the missing or sadness shows up, notice it.
  2. Give it five minutes: breathe, feel it, maybe write a line.
  3. Then go back to your day on purpose.

If you get stuck

If five minutes feels like too much, three slow breaths with it count.

In real life made-up example

Yara left a job she’d outgrown and missed her team more than she expected. On Monday the missing hit on her drive in. She let it be there for five minutes, no podcast. Then she parked, walked in, and made her first call at the new place.

Leave the space open

2 min

For the next 7 days, don’t refill the space you made with something like the old thing. Each evening, write down anything new that showed up.

Why it helps

Empty space feels uncomfortable, so it’s tempting to fill it with something that looks like the old thing. Leaving it open a while gives something new a chance to show up.

Walk me through it

  1. For the next 7 days, don’t rush to replace what you let go.
  2. Each evening, write anything new you noticed: an idea, a person, a pull.
  3. At the end of the week, read the list back.

If you get stuck

If the space feels too empty, fill it with rest, not with a copy of the old thing.

In real life made-up example

Dom left a relationship and felt the urge to download the dating apps that same night. He didn’t. For a week he wrote down what showed up instead: a pottery class he’d always wanted to try, an old friend who called, a weekend he enjoyed alone. By Sunday the space felt less like a gap and more like room.

Tools

11 steps

The Tuesday test

5 min

For five evenings, write down: your low point, your high point, how long it took to recover, whether you let yourself feel it, and whether you did your step.

Why it helps

You can’t see your patterns from inside a single day. Five evenings of short notes show how you really move through highs, lows and recovery.

Walk me through it

  1. Each evening for five days, write your low point and high point.
  2. Write how long it took you to recover from the low.
  3. Write whether you let yourself feel it, and whether you did your step.

If you get stuck

If five days feels long, do three. Even three evenings show a pattern.

In real life made-up example

Monica did the Tuesday test for five nights. Her lows were mostly about work emails. Her recovery time ranged from ten minutes to all evening. The fast ones were the nights she let herself be annoyed for a minute instead of pretending she was fine. That was the pattern she needed.

What really changed

10 min

List three things that really changed in your life in recent years. For each, mark where it came from: mostly the wanting, or mostly small steps repeated.

Why it helps

It’s easy to believe change comes from wanting hard enough. Looking at what really changed in your life shows what actually worked.

Walk me through it

  1. List three real changes in your life from recent years.
  2. For each, mark where it came from: mostly the wanting, or mostly small steps repeated.
  3. Look at what that tells you about how you change.

If you get stuck

If it’s both, mark both. That’s useful too.

In real life made-up example

Aaron listed: paid off his car, mostly steps. Got closer with his dad, both. Lost 20 pounds, steps. He’d always felt like the guy who didn’t want things badly enough. His own list said he was the guy who changes through small, boring steps. He stopped waiting to feel motivated.

The data debrief

10 min

After a Try, answer in this order: How do I feel about it? What actually happened? What did I learn about what I like and what I’m good at? What would I do differently? What good came out of it, even if it went sideways?

Why it helps

A Try only teaches you something if you look at it. Answering the same questions, in order, turns any outcome into useful information.

Walk me through it

  1. How do I feel about it?
  2. What actually happened?
  3. What did I learn about what I like and what I’m good at? What would I do differently?
  4. What good came out of it, even if it went sideways?

If you get stuck

If it went badly and you don’t want to look, answer just the first and last questions.

In real life made-up example

Simone taught three yoga classes as a Try. Her debrief: felt flat afterward. Three classes, small crowds. She liked teaching, not the admin. Next time she’d co-teach. Good part: she found out she’s calm in front of a room. She’d never have learned that without trying.

Make a gut call

3 min

If a step feels wrong in your gut, skip or change it on purpose. Write what you changed and what your gut said.

Why it helps

Your gut often knows before your head does. Skipping or changing a step on purpose, and writing why, honors that knowing without losing the thread.

Walk me through it

  1. Notice when a step feels wrong in your gut.
  2. Skip it or change it on purpose. Don’t just drift.
  3. Write one line: what you changed and what your gut said.

If you get stuck

If you can’t tell gut from fear, write it down anyway. The check-back will tell you.

In real life made-up example

Ryan’s plan said to pitch a cold client. His gut said no, not this one. He skipped it and wrote: “Skipped the pitch. Gut said wrong client, not wrong timing.” He pitched a different client that week. He found that writing down the reason made it feel like a choice, not avoidance.

The gut check-back

5 min

Two weeks after a gut call, look back: how did it play out? Was it intuition, or fear in disguise?

Why it helps

Gut calls get sharper when you check how they played out. Looking back helps you learn the difference between intuition and fear in disguise.

Walk me through it

  1. Two weeks after a gut call, look at what you wrote.
  2. Ask: how did it play out?
  3. Ask: was it intuition, or fear dressed up as intuition?

If you get stuck

If you can’t tell, that’s okay. Keep checking back on calls, and your read will sharpen.

In real life made-up example

Kara looked back at two gut calls. Skipping the networking event: right call, it was a bad fit. Skipping the gym three times: fear, she’d been nervous about a new class. Both felt like gut in the moment. Now she knows what her fear feels like when it sounds wise.

The word check

5 min

Rewrite your Ask or today’s check-in so every word is exact and true.

Why it helps

Loose words make loose results. When your Ask is exact and true, your mind knows what to do and when it’s done.

Walk me through it

  1. Write your Ask or today’s check-in.
  2. Circle any loose words: try, more, better, soon, someday.
  3. Rewrite them as exact and true: what, how much, by when.

If you get stuck

If exact feels scary, start with one word. Swap “soon” for a real date.

In real life made-up example

Jamal’s Ask was “I’ll try to save more.” He rewrote it: “I move $50 to savings every Friday.” The new version felt smaller and much more real. He set up the transfer that afternoon. Six Fridays later he had $300, which “trying to save more” had never produced.

Log a test

2 min

When something tests your new level, write one line: what happened, and whether you met it with your old reaction or a new response.

Why it helps

New levels get tested. Logging each test, and whether you met it the old way or the new way, shows you how much you’ve actually changed.

Walk me through it

  1. When something tests your new level, notice it.
  2. Write one line: what happened.
  3. Write whether you met it with your old reaction or a new response.

If you get stuck

If you reacted the old way, write it down anyway. That’s information, not a verdict.

In real life made-up example

After leaving a draining job, Lily’s old boss called with a bigger offer. Old Lily would have said yes to avoid disappointing him. She wrote: “Old boss offered more money. New response: I said no, kindly.” Seeing it in writing made it real that she’d changed.

The flow log

3 min

Each evening, write one thing that came easily today and one thing that fought you. After two weeks, read it back and look for the pattern.

Why it helps

Flow and resistance leave clues every day. A short nightly log turns those clues into a pattern you can actually follow.

Walk me through it

  1. Each evening, write one thing that came easily today.
  2. Write one thing that fought you.
  3. After two weeks, read it back and look for the pattern.

If you get stuck

If you miss a night, just write the next one. The pattern doesn’t need every day.

In real life made-up example

Victor kept a flow log for two weeks. Easy: client calls, every time. Fought him: the spreadsheet, every time. He’d been forcing himself to do the books and dreading calls. The log showed it was the other way around. He hired a bookkeeper for four hours a month.

Ease or force?

5 min

Pick one Ask, path or person. Over the last 2 to 4 weeks, have doors mostly opened or mostly closed? Has it given you energy or taken it?

Why it helps

Some things open doors and give you energy. Others hit wall after wall. Looking at a few weeks honestly tells you which is which.

Walk me through it

  1. Pick one Ask, path or person.
  2. Look back over the last two to four weeks.
  3. Ask: have doors mostly opened or mostly closed? Has it given me energy or taken it?

If you get stuck

If it’s mixed, that’s normal. Look at the overall direction, not every single day.

In real life made-up example

Nora looked at her two side projects. The podcast: guests kept saying yes, and she looked forward to recording. Flow. The online store: supplier problems, slow sales, dread every week. Resistance. She didn’t quit the store, but she put her extra hours into the podcast.

Follow the open door

10 min

Notice something that keeps opening up for you, even when you’re not pushing. Take one small step toward it.

Why it helps

Sometimes the clearest sign is the thing that keeps showing up without you pushing. Taking one step toward it is how you find out if it’s meant for you.

Walk me through it

  1. Notice what keeps opening up for you, again and again.
  2. Write it down in one line.
  3. Take one small step toward it.

If you get stuck

If you’re not sure what keeps showing up, ask a friend. They often see it first.

In real life made-up example

People kept asking Hector to teach what he did with woodworking. He’d always said “I’m not a teacher.” He booked one free workshop for four people at his garage. It was awkward and fun, and two of them asked when the next one was.

The arrival review

15 min

When you reach your Ask, write what changed, what it drains and amplifies, how it lands on the people around you, and what it costs to keep.

Why it helps

Getting what you wanted changes things, some for the better and some at a cost. Reviewing it honestly helps you keep what matters.

Walk me through it

  1. When you reach your Ask, set aside fifteen minutes.
  2. Write what changed, what it drains and what it amplifies.
  3. Write how it lands on the people around you, and what it costs to keep.

If you get stuck

If it feels ungrateful to look at the costs, remember: seeing the cost is how you protect the win.

In real life made-up example

Rebecca got the promotion she’d worked toward for two years. Her review: more money, less sleep. Her partner was proud, her kids missed her on Saturdays. Keeping it meant one Saturday off a month, protected. She asked for it, and got it.
