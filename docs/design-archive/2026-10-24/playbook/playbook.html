The rules every plan follows

How a plan is built

Three sets of rules sit above every step: the shape of a week, how it sounds, and the lines it never crosses.

The shape of a week

How a 7-day plan is put together

  1. Every plan is built around the person’s Ask: the one thing they want. Examples are tailored to it; titles stay as written.
  2. Most days build their other half: the side they use least. One or two days build the second-weakest side.
  3. Day 1 is the easiest step in the plan. Start small so the first day is a win.
  4. One stretch step per week, a little past comfortable. For people who lean on wanting, that’s an action; for people who lean on effort, it’s rest or a hand-off.
  5. Day 4 is the shadow question for their type, in place of a step.
  6. Day 7 is a 5-minute look back: what you did, what changed, what to keep for next week.
  7. Every day has a small version. A small version done counts as a day done.
  8. No step takes more than 20 minutes. Most take 10 or less.

Your voice

How every plan sounds

  1. Plain words. Short sentences. Every line understood in seconds.
  2. Name the real issue first, but say how it feels before saying what to do. Never blame.
  3. Warm and blunt. Celebrate small wins; praise only what they actually did.
  4. No hype, no fake urgency, no "abundance", "vibration" or "universe" filler.
  5. No diminishing words like "grind" or "hustle". If their own words use them, quote them.
  6. Never say "failed". A step not done is information, not a verdict.
  7. Offer, don’t command. They know their life better than any method. If their gut says no, they can skip a step and note why.
  8. Never invent stories about Jarred. Use only stories he has supplied.
  9. Use the app’s words for the method: a good, okay or hard day; the small version; one line on today; days done; plays. Never “receipt”, “full, half or rough”, “rough-day version”, “kept” for days, or “protocol”.

Never

The hard rules

  1. Coaching and reflection, not therapy or medical advice. Never diagnose.
  2. If anything they write suggests crisis, self-harm or danger, stop the plan and show crisis resources instead.
  3. Never promise outcomes. No "you will manifest", no guarantees about money, love or health.
  4. Never push past safety: no fasting, extreme exercise, quitting medication, or big money moves as steps.
  5. Only use steps and questions from this playbook. Tailor the examples, never the method.
  6. Keep it belief-neutral: it can be read as energetic, psychological, or just how people change.

Ebb and flow

How plans move with real life

  1. Flow, not force. A plan is a direction, not a timetable.
  2. There’s no set time. Any moment today counts. If your usual moment falls through, find another one before bed.
  3. Each day starts with a check-in: a good, okay or hard day? Do the version of the step that matches.
  4. Low motivation, a foggy head, a hard day, no creativity: those are real. Shrink the step, don’t skip it.
  5. Missed a day? Don’t make it up. No catch-up debt. Pick up today’s step, and try not to miss twice in a row.
  6. If a certain day or time keeps not working, move the step. Your plan learns your rhythm.
  7. Ebb isn’t quitting. On the low days, still do one thing that moves your situation, even if it takes two minutes.
  8. Creativity dried up? Change the shape, the place or the time. Keep the goal.
  9. Match the task to your steam. Forcing creative or important work on an empty tank often means doing it twice. On low days, do the prep work instead: plan, gather, tidy, rest.
  10. Save the deep work for when you’re in flow, and learn when that tends to be. But don’t wait forever: if the right moment hasn’t come in two days, do the smallest version anyway.

Let loose, be precise

Living lightly

  1. Let loose. When things get really hard, find the laugh before the anger. Lightness is a choice you can make right in the middle of the mess.
  2. Laughing doesn’t mean it isn’t hard. Feel it first, then loosen your grip on it.
  3. Anger and pressure build when you hold on tight. A laugh lets some of it out before it piles up.
  4. Hold your Ask with a light grip. You’re here to enjoy your life, not just to improve it.
  5. Pressure isn’t proof you care. Effort can be light and still count. If every day feels like “grinding”, something needs to get lighter.
  6. Not everything is serious. Ask: will this matter in a year? If not, let it be small.
  7. Play is part of the plan. Laughing, moving, making something for fun: that’s fuel, not a break from the work.
  8. Be precise about the few things that matter: your words, your Ask, and what you let into your field. Let the rest be loose.

Protocol

Building a new habit

Use itWhen their Ask is something they want to do regularly: move, write, save, wake earlier.

  1. Name it as who you’re becoming, plus one tiny action: “I’m someone who moves every day. Shoes on, ten minutes.”
  2. Shrink it until it survives a Tuesday. If it feels too small, it’s probably the right size.
  3. Anchor it to something you already do, not to a clock: after coffee, after the school run, before your phone.
  4. Write three versions: for a good day, an okay day and a hard day. All three count.
  5. Do it most days, at whatever moment works that day.
  6. Write one line a day about what you actually did.
  7. Missed a day? No catch-up. Come back the next day, and try not to miss twice in a row.
  8. Every 7 days, look back: keep it, shrink it or change its shape. Grow it one notch only when it feels easy most days.

Protocol

Unlearning an old habit

Use itWhen their Ask is about stopping something that’s costing them: scrolling, snacking, procrastinating, overworking.

  1. Notice without judging. For three days, write down when it happens: what set it off, how you felt, what you got from it.
  2. Find the payoff. Ask: what am I getting out of this? Relief, comfort, a break, a sense of control.
  3. Make the old way a little harder. Move it, log out, put it in another room, add a step between you and it.
  4. Choose a swap that gives you the same payoff in a better way: a walk for the break, a text to a friend for the comfort.
  5. When the urge shows up, pause for two minutes and feel it before you choose. Urges rise, and they pass.
  6. A slip is information. Write what set it off and what would help next time, then carry on.
  7. Tell one person in your circle what you’re changing, and ask them to check in.
  8. Count the days you chose differently, not the days you didn’t.

SafetyIf the habit involves alcohol, drugs, gambling, food, self-harm or anything that puts you in danger, this doesn’t replace real help. Talk to a doctor or counselor. If you’re in crisis, call your local emergency number or a crisis line now.

Protocol

Clearing your field

Use itWhen something in their life keeps pulling against their Ask: a person, a habit, a food, a feed, a place, a commitment. Let loose, but be precise about what you let in.

  1. Take stock of what you let in: people, places, habits, food, feeds, commitments. Mark each one: gives energy, drains it, or neither.
  2. For each drain, ask: does this help my Ask or cost it? Be honest, not harsh.
  3. Choose the gentlest change that works: less of it, a boundary around it, or letting it go. Most things need a boundary, not a slammed door.
  4. With people, start with a kind, clear boundary: “I can’t talk after 9.” Distance comes before goodbye. With anyone close, think it through first, and never cut someone off in the heat of the moment.
  5. With habits and food, swap, don’t starve. Change one thing at a time and replace it with something that gives you what it gave you. No crash diets, no extreme cuts.
  6. Clear one small space at a time: one drawer, one app, one feed. Unfollow, mute, delete.
  7. Fill the space you made with something that feeds your Ask. Empty space fills back up with the old thing.
  8. Letting go has a price too. Grief or guilt can show up, even for the right change. After 7 days, check in: lighter, or lonelier? Adjust from there.

SafetyIf someone in your life is unsafe or harming you, get support from someone you trust or a professional before you act, and if you’re in danger, call your local emergency number or a domestic violence hotline. Before big changes to food, alcohol, medication or other substances, talk to a doctor.

Protocol

The Price check: when it gets hard

Use itWhen they’ve stalled, pushed back or missed several days, or they say the Ask feels wrong. The Price stage of their Ask.

  1. Pause before deciding. Feel what’s there first: tired, scared, bored, or something deeper.
  2. Remember that resistance doesn’t automatically mean it’s wrong. Friction is part of how results happen.
  3. Ask: is this friction or a signal? Friction sounds like “this is hard, and I still want it.” A signal sounds like “this costs me more than it gives,” every time.
  4. Look back over the last 2 to 4 weeks: have doors been opening, or closing again and again? (See “Reading flow and resistance”.)
  5. Answer the Price question again: what will this really cost me, and will I still pay it on a bad day?
  6. If it’s friction, keep going, and shrink the step until it fits.
  7. If it’s a signal, choose one: change the shape (same goal, different way), not now (pick a date to look again), or let it go with acceptance.
  8. Whatever you choose, write one line on why. That’s data, not a verdict.
  9. Letting go with acceptance isn’t giving up. It makes room for the next Ask.

Protocol

Tries and the data debrief

Use itWhen they don’t know yet what they want, or want to test something before committing: a career, a hobby, a business idea, a new kind of relationship. Try more things sooner.

  1. Decide whether your Ask is a Build (you know you want it) or a Try (you want to find out).
  2. Give a Try a set length: 2 to 4 weeks. Long enough to learn, short enough to start now.
  3. Pick what you’ll notice along the way: what you enjoy, what comes naturally, what drains you.
  4. Do it for real, with small daily steps like any Ask.
  5. At the end, do the data debrief. Feel it first, then find the data.
  6. Decide: turn it into a Build, run another Try, or close it and move on.
  7. Add what you learned to your “What I’m learning about me” list.
  8. Count every Try as a win. Each one moves you closer to what’s yours.

Protocol

Gut calls

Use itWhen a step, or the whole plan, feels wrong in their gut.

  1. You know your life better than any method, this one included. If your gut says no, you can skip or change a step.
  2. Make it a gut call on purpose: write what you’re skipping or changing, and what your gut is saying.
  3. Then follow your gut fully. No half-measures.
  4. In two weeks, look back: how did it play out?
  5. Ask: was that my intuition, or fear in disguise? Intuition tends to feel calm and clear. Fear tends to feel urgent and tight.
  6. Keep a gut record. Over time you’ll see when your gut is right, and when it’s protecting you from growing.
  7. No shame either way. A gut call that went sideways is still data.

Protocol

Receiving it

Use itWhen they reach their Ask, or a big part of it. The Receive stage of their Ask.

  1. Stop and mark it. Say out loud what you did to get here.
  2. On the day: write what changed in your life, your days, your body, your money and your relationships.
  3. Notice what it drains and what it amplifies. More energy or less? More peace or more pressure?
  4. Ask how it lands on the people around you: does it help them, cost them, or both?
  5. Name what it costs to keep it. Every new level has upkeep.
  6. At 30 days and again at 90 days, answer the same questions and compare.
  7. Decide what to keep, adjust or let go. Then choose your next Ask when you’re ready, not before.

Protocol

Holding the new level

Use itAfter they reach an Ask, while life tests whether the change will stick. The Hold stage of their Ask.

  1. Expect tests. A new level gets tested, often by the same thing in a new form.
  2. Pick your own word for where tests come from: life, God, the universe, or just “things that happen”. The app will use yours.
  3. When a test shows up, log it in one line: what happened.
  4. Then ask: did I meet it with my old reaction, or a new response?
  5. Old reaction? No shame. Notice what set it off, and what the new response would have been.
  6. New response? Mark it. That’s the new level holding.
  7. Watch for the same test coming back in a new form. Spotting it is half of handling it.
  8. Each level asks something new of you: more time, more boundaries, more help. Update your load map and your circle.

Protocol

Be precise: the word check

Use itWhenever they write their Ask or a check-in. The other half of “let loose”: live lightly, but be exact about the words you use for what matters.

  1. Read your Ask out loud. Does every word say exactly what you mean?
  2. Watch for loose words: “try to”, “maybe”, “I hope”, “someday”, “I never”, “I always”, “I can’t”.
  3. Rewrite it to be exact and true. Not fake-positive, just precise. “I’ll try to run more” becomes “I run 10 minutes, 4 days a week.”
  4. Name the thing, the amount and the when, if you can.
  5. Say what you want, not only what you don’t. “Stop being broke” becomes “Have $2,000 saved by June.”
  6. Swap “I never” and “I always” for what’s actually true: “I haven’t yet” or “I often”.
  7. If the precise version feels scary to say, keep it anyway. Precise is a kind of honesty.

Protocol

When you feel alone

Use itWhen they feel lonely, disconnected, or like no one gets them, even with people around. The other side of “When it’s too much”.

  1. Name it without shame. Loneliness is a signal, like hunger: it means you need connection, not that something is wrong with you.
  2. Notice which kind it is: missing closeness with someone, missing a group, or feeling unseen even around people.
  3. Start small: one message to one person. “Thought of you today.”
  4. Be around people with no pressure: a café, a class, a gym, a walk where others are.
  5. Show up somewhere on a regular basis. Closeness grows from seeing the same faces again.
  6. Let one person in a little more: share something real, and ask them something real back.
  7. Check: solitude or isolation? Solitude is chosen and recharges you. Isolation drifts in and drains you.

SafetyIf loneliness turns into hopelessness, or thoughts of not wanting to be here, reach out now: to someone you trust, a doctor, or a crisis line. You don’t have to carry that alone.

Protocol

When it’s too much

Use itWhen people, noise, screens, plans or other people’s needs leave them overloaded, wired or snappy. The other side of “When you feel alone”.

  1. Notice the signs: snappy, foggy, wired but tired, wanting everyone to stop talking.
  2. Take a quiet reset: ten minutes with no input. No phone, no talk, no noise.
  3. Cut one input today: notifications off, one plan moved, one group chat muted.
  4. Say a kind no to one ask: “I can’t this week, but thank you for thinking of me.”
  5. Protect your recharge: put alone time on your calendar like a meeting.
  6. Use your senses to come down: a walk outside, a shower, dim lights, slow music.
  7. Find your mix. Everyone needs a different amount of people and quiet. Notice yours, and plan your week around it.
  8. Too much can tip into too little. If quiet turns into hiding, use “When you feel alone”.

Protocol

Opening doors: new people, new opportunities

Use itWhen their Ask needs people or chances they don’t have yet: work, clients, love, friends, mentors, ideas, money. Opportunities come in every form.

  1. Decide which doors you want opened: work, love, friendship, mentors, ideas, money.
  2. Tell people your Ask. Opportunities find people who say out loud what they’re looking for.
  3. Walk into one new room a month: an event, a class, a group, a community online or in person, where people care about what you care about.
  4. Give first. Share a lead, make an introduction, help with something small. Giving opens more doors than asking.
  5. Reach out to one new or old contact a week. Keep it simple and real.
  6. Follow up. Most doors open on the second or third conversation, not the first.
  7. Say yes to an invite you’d usually skip, unless your gut says no.
  8. Ask for one introduction: “Who do you know who…?”
  9. Keep your field clear while it grows. New people are welcome, and your boundaries still count.

Protocol

Reading flow and resistance

Use itWhen they’re unsure whether an Ask, a path or a person is meant for them, or everything feels like pushing uphill.

  1. Hard and wrong aren’t the same thing. Effort you choose can still feel like flow. What you’re watching is the direction, not the difficulty.
  2. Look for the signs of flow: doors open, people say yes, things line up, you lose track of time, and your energy comes back even after hard days.
  3. Look for the signs of resistance: every step is a fight, doors keep closing, the same block keeps showing up, and it drains you more than it gives.
  4. Give it a fair run before you read it. One bad week isn’t a pattern. Look back over 2 to 4 weeks.
  5. Read people the same way. The people meant for you tend to feel easy to be around, even in hard conversations. Constant friction with someone is information.
  6. If it’s mostly flow, lean in. Put more of your time and energy where things open up.
  7. If it’s non-stop resistance, ask what it’s telling you: not this, not now, or not this way. Then use the Price check to choose.
  8. Follow the open door. Sometimes what’s meant for you is the thing that keeps showing up while you’re pushing on something else.

Protocol

The pause before yes

Use itWhen they say yes too fast to keep someone happy, before they realize what it will cost them, especially when their own cup is already empty.

  1. Notice the urge to say yes on the spot. A fast yes to keep the peace is a habit, not a decision.
  2. Buy time: “Let me check and get back to you.” A real yes can wait an hour.
  3. Check your cup: is your time, energy or money full, half or empty right now?
  4. Ask the price: what will this yes really cost me, and what will I have to say no to because of it?
  5. Choose on purpose: yes, a smaller yes, a later, or a kind no.
  6. If it’s a no, keep it short and kind. You don’t owe a long explanation.
  7. It’s okay to change a yes you gave too fast. Go back early and offer what you can actually do.
  8. Filling your own cup first isn’t selfish. It’s how you keep having something to give.

Protocol

Your own agency

Use itWhen they keep pleasing people who take advantage of them, or act differently than they normally would when a certain person or thing is around.

  1. Notice where you shrink: with whom, or around what, do you stop doing what you’d normally do or say?
  2. Name what you would do if they weren’t there. That’s your own agency talking.
  3. Check whether it flows both ways. Do they give as well as take? Is your no respected, or argued with until it turns into a yes?
  4. Watch for the signs of being taken advantage of: guilt used to move you, help that only goes one way, and plans that always run on their terms.
  5. Take one small act of agency in their presence: order what you want, say what you think, leave when you’re ready.
  6. Set one clear boundary, and decide what you’ll do if it isn’t respected.
  7. Let them be disappointed. Someone else’s disappointment isn’t your emergency.
  8. Tell someone in your circle what you’re changing. Agency is easier with backup.

SafetyIf you feel unsafe saying no to someone, or they control your money, where you go or who you see, that isn’t a boundary to solve alone. Reach out to someone you trust, a counselor, or a domestic violence hotline. If you’re in danger, call your local emergency number.

Protocol

Learning to say no

Use itWhen saying no feels hard, scary or rude, so they say yes to things they don’t want, can’t afford, or don’t have room for.

  1. No is a full sentence, and a kind no is still a no.
  2. Start small. Write a “no ladder” from easiest to hardest, and practice on the easy ones first: a waiter, a sales call, one extra task.
  3. Say no to the thing, not the person: “I can’t take this on, and I appreciate you asking.”
  4. Keep it short. The more you explain, the more there is to argue with.
  5. If they push, say it again, calmly, with the same words.
  6. Expect a guilt wave afterward. Feel it, and don’t take the no back.
  7. Notice what your no made room for: time, rest, your Ask.
  8. Say no to yourself too: to the old habit, the extra scroll, the late night that costs you tomorrow.

Protocol

Speaking up for yourself

Use itWhen they swallow what they think or need, go along to avoid conflict, or hint instead of asking.

  1. Notice the moment you swallow it: the thought you didn’t say, the need you didn’t mention.
  2. Start where it’s easy. Speak up in low-stakes moments first: a wrong order, the seat you want, your take on a movie.
  3. Use “I” statements: “I see it differently.” “I need a day to think.” “I’d prefer…”
  4. Say it early, while it’s small. Swallowed things grow.
  5. Keep your voice calm and your words simple. You don’t have to be loud to be clear.
  6. Ask for what you need directly instead of hinting.
  7. If it comes out wobbly, it still counts. You can circle back: “I want to say that more clearly.”
  8. Not everyone will like it. The people meant for you will respect it.

Protocol

Your worth, held

Use itWhen they doubt themselves, feel they have to earn their place, or don’t believe they deserve what they’re asking for.

  1. Worth is held, not hyped. Telling yourself “I’m worthy” a hundred times moves less than keeping one small promise to yourself for twenty days.
  2. Keep small promises to yourself. Every kept one is proof your gut can read.
  3. Write your proof list: things you’ve done, handled, or made it through. Add to it every week.
  4. Notice your inner critic. Write down what it says, then what you’d say to a friend who said that about themselves.
  5. Drop the words that shrink you: “just”, “only”, “sorry”, “I’m no expert”.
  6. Stop earning your worth by overgiving. Your value isn’t measured by what you do for others.
  7. Take up space on purpose: let a compliment land, name your price, sit where you want to sit.
  8. Measure yourself against who you were last month, not against someone else’s highlight reel.

SafetyIf your sense of worth is very low, or it comes with hopelessness, talk to a counselor or doctor. Worth can be rebuilt, and you don’t have to do it alone. If you’re in crisis, call your local emergency number or a crisis line now.

Protocol

Catching error thoughts

Use itWhen harsh thoughts or statements about themselves keep showing up, including ones they picked up from other people. Unlearning what they tell themselves.

  1. Watch for the moments. Old thoughts show up when you’re stuck, tired, comparing yourself, or something goes wrong.
  2. Catch it as it happens. Notice the exact words: “I’m stupid.” “I always mess this up.” “People like me don’t get that.”
  3. Name it, out loud or in your head: “Error thought.” Naming it puts space between you and it.
  4. Ask whose voice it is. Many of these started as something someone else said to you. You don’t have to keep carrying it.
  5. Flip it to the inverse, in words you can believe. “I’m stupid” becomes “This is tough, but I’ll figure it out.”
  6. Make the flip true, not fake. If the full opposite feels like a lie, use a bridge: “I’m learning this.” “I’m getting better at this.”
  7. Back it up with one small action. Your gut believes what you do, so the action is what makes the new thought stick.
  8. Catch what you say out loud too. “I’m terrible with money” in a conversation is an error statement. Correct it mid-sentence.
  9. Keep an error log. Within a few weeks you’ll see the same few error thoughts repeat, and you’ll catch them faster.

Protocol

Your five: the family you’re building

Use itWhen they want to grow, and the people closest to them shape what feels normal. The few people you spend the most time with shape your habits, moods and chances. Research on social networks shows habits like exercise, eating, smoking and even happiness spread between people who are close, and we naturally mirror the people around us most.

  1. Map your five: the people you spend the most time and contact with, in person, on the phone and online.
  2. For each one, notice what you pick up from them: habits, moods, and how you both talk about money, work, your body and your future. We mirror the people closest to us, the good and the bad.
  3. Ask of each: does time with them pull me toward my Ask, or away from it? Do I leave amplified, drained, or neutral?
  4. Name the people you want more of: people already living part of what you want, who make you better just by being around them.
  5. Grow your circle on purpose. Spend more time where those people are: groups, classes, communities, work. (See “Opening doors”.)
  6. Rebalance, don’t purge. A little less time with the people who drain you, a little more with the people who amplify you. Distance comes before goodbye.
  7. Be the friend you want to have. Bring the habits, encouragement and honesty you’re looking for. Like attracts like.
  8. Build your chosen family: the few people who show up, tell you the truth, and want you to win. Invest in them on purpose.
  9. Check your five every few months. As you change, your circle will change too, and that’s part of growing.

Four ways to stay clear

Use itFor everyday life, especially in hard moments with other people: mean what you say, their world not your worth, ask don’t guess, and your best for the day you have.

  1. Mean what you say. Your word counts: say what you mean, keep your promises, and don’t use words against yourself or anyone else. (See “Be precise” and “Catching error thoughts”.)
  2. Their world, not your worth. What people say and do mostly comes from what they’re carrying, not from your value.
  3. When something stings, pause before you react and ask: is this about me, or about their world?
  4. Ask, don’t guess. Instead of filling in the blanks, ask: “What did you mean by that?”
  5. Say what you want clearly, so other people don’t have to guess either.
  6. Your best, for the day you have. Your best changes day to day, and a hard-day best still counts. No guilt, no burnout.
  7. At night, check in: Did I mean what I said? Did I let someone else’s words or mood decide how I felt about myself? Did I guess instead of asking? Did I give my best for the day I had?

The comeback

Use itWhen they’ve fallen off completely: a lost week, a lost month, or longer.

  1. Welcome back. No shame and no catch-up debt. Starting again is the skill.
  2. Don’t audit the gap. Skip the guilt review; all you need is the next step.
  3. Restart with the small version of your step for three days.
  4. Ask one question about the gap: what pulled me away? Write one line, then move on.
  5. Remove one thing that made falling off easy.
  6. Tell one person you’re back, if that helps you stay.
  7. Count from today. How fast you come back matters more than any streak.

Protocol

Body basics: fuel your steam

Use itWhen low energy, poor sleep or skipped meals keep shrinking their steam.

  1. Your steam starts in your body. Sleep, movement, food and water come before willpower.
  2. Sleep: pick one thing that helps you rest, like a set wind-down time or the phone out of the bedroom.
  3. Move a little every day, any way you enjoy. A ten-minute walk counts.
  4. Eat something real before you run on empty. Regular meals beat perfect ones.
  5. Drink water before coffee.
  6. Get outside and see daylight early in the day.
  7. Change one basic at a time, and notice what it does for your steam.

SafetyThis is everyday care, not medical advice. For ongoing exhaustion, sleep problems, pain, or big changes to food or exercise, talk to a doctor.

Protocol

Big life changes

Use itDuring a move, a breakup, a job loss, a new baby, an illness or a loss, when life itself has become the big Ask.

  1. Big changes take energy, even the good ones. Lower the bar on purpose.
  2. Switch your plan to gentle mode: small versions, fewer of them, more rest.
  3. Keep one small anchor that’s the same every day: a walk, a meal, a call.
  4. Let yourself feel it. Grief, fear and relief can all show up at once.
  5. Lean on your circle, and ask for specific help: a meal, a ride, a call.
  6. Put big decisions on hold where you can, until the ground feels steadier.
  7. When you’re ready, ask what this change makes room for. That can become your next Ask.

Protocol

Your signal: feel it, hold it, live it

Use itAt the heart of every Ask. What you hold in your gut most of the day is your signal. Words and wishes don’t change it; feelings faced and actions taken do.

Energy lensFor people who see it as energy: everything is energy, like attracts like, and you are a broadcaster and receiver of what you feel. Your signal is your frequency, and the steps tune it.

Mind lensFor people who see it as psychology: what you feel shapes what you notice, what you choose and how others respond to you. Your signal is your baseline, and the steps shift it.

  1. Your signal is what you hold, not what you say. Repeating words over a feeling you don’t have hides the feeling instead of changing it.
  2. Feel the future fully. Picture an ordinary day once your Ask is real, in first person, as if it’s already true. Notice how it feels in your body.
  3. Notice the gap. What shows up right after? Doubt, fear, “that’s not for me”. That’s the other half. Don’t cover it; feel it.
  4. Ask the shadow question that fits: the Price, the Payoff, or the Fear on the far side.
  5. Choose one feeling from that future to hold today: calm, steady, enough. Held, not high. A spike fades; a baseline changes things.
  6. Live it in one small way. Make one choice today that the version of you who already has it would make. Action teaches your body the future is normal.
  7. Check your signal at 2:47. Are you holding it, or did old feelings take over? No shame. Come back to it.
  8. Notice what’s moving: doors opening, people showing up, small signs. Be grateful for what’s already on its way.
  9. Let go of the how and the when. Hold the feeling, take the step, and loosen your grip on the rest.

Protocol

Navigating your emotions

Use itWhen a big feeling hits, or the same heavy feeling keeps coming back and pulls them away from their Ask.

  1. Name it, as exactly as you can: not “bad”, but “disappointed”, “embarrassed”, “scared I’m too late”. Naming a feeling takes some of its power.
  2. Find it in your body: the tight chest, the knot in your stomach, the heat in your face.
  3. Feel it fully for a minute or two, without fixing it. Feelings move through faster when you let them.
  4. Ask what it’s telling you. Fear often points at something that matters. Anger often points at a boundary.
  5. Move one step up, not ten. From hopeless to frustrated, from frustrated to hopeful. Pick the next feeling you can actually believe.
  6. Act from the new feeling, in one small way.
  7. If it’s an old heavy feeling that keeps coming back, notice what sets it off, and use “Catching error thoughts” on the thought that rides with it.
  8. Don’t bottle it and don’t fake it. Felt and moved through beats pushed down and covered up.

SafetyIf a feeling is too big to move through, or it stays for weeks, talk to someone you trust or a counselor. If you feel unsafe or hopeless, call your local emergency number or a crisis line now.

Protocol

Keeping your power

Use itWhen they believe someone else holds all the keys, or that they have to do it exactly the way someone else did. Also when they’re taking advice from mentors, coaches, books or courses.

  1. Notice where you hand your power over: “I can’t until they…”, “If I just find the right coach, course or person…”, “I have to do it exactly like they did.”
  2. No one holds all the keys. People can open doors and share what worked for them, but your path won’t look like theirs.
  3. There’s no single right way. Everyone is different: a different life, timing, set of strengths and starting point.
  4. Learn from people who’ve done it, and keep your hands on the wheel. Advice is input, not orders.
  5. Run advice through your gut. What resonates? Keep it. What feels off? Leave it, without needing to argue about it.
  6. Test a piece of advice as a Try, not a rule. See whether it works for you before you make it yours.
  7. Be careful with anyone who says theirs is the only way, or makes you feel you can’t do it without them.
  8. Take credit for your own progress. When something works, notice what you did.
  9. That includes this app. Take what fits, change what doesn’t, and trust yourself.

SafetyIf a coach, group or person pressures you to keep paying more, to cut off the people close to you, or to hand over control of your choices to keep your progress, step back and talk it through with someone you trust.

Protocol

Making room: letting go of the old to let the new in

Use itWhen you’re holding on to something that’s already over: an old chapter, an old version of yourself, an old hurt, or something you’ve put years into. You can’t fully take in the new while your hands are full of the old.

  1. Name what you’re still carrying: a role, a label, a story, a hurt, a hope, a thing you keep paying for. Write it down. Seeing it is the first loosening.
  2. Honor it before you release it. Write what it gave you, and thank it. Letting go isn’t pretending it didn’t matter.
  3. Retire one old label. “I’m the responsible one.” “I’m not a creative person.” Write who you’re becoming instead, in words you can believe.
  4. Stop paying for the past. If you wouldn’t start it today, ask what keeping it costs you now. Years already spent aren’t a reason to spend more.
  5. Forgive to free yourself. Forgiving isn’t excusing what happened or letting someone back in. It’s putting down a weight you’ve been carrying for them.
  6. Let grief come. Even the right goodbye can hurt. Feel it in small doses, then go back to your day.
  7. Leave the space open for a while. Don’t rush to fill it with something that looks like the old thing. Notice what starts to show up.
  8. After 7 days, look back: what feels lighter, and what wants to come in?

SafetyIf what you’re holding on to involves abuse, trauma or a deep loss, you don’t have to work through it alone. Talk to someone you trust or a professional. Forgiving someone never means staying somewhere unsafe. If you’re in crisis, call your local emergency number or a crisis line now.
